## Problem

Buses arrive as a Poisson process with rate $\lambda = 0.1$ per minute (one every 10 minutes on average), and have been running for a long time. You arrive at a random time. What is your expected waiting time? On average, how long ago did the previous bus leave?

## Solution

1. Gaps are i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$: $P(\tau > t) = e^{-\lambda t}$, mean $\frac1\lambda = 10$.
2. **Memoryless:** however long it has been since the last bus,
$$P(\tau > s+t\mid \tau > s) = \frac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} = e^{-\lambda t},$$
so your wait is $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$: mean **10 minutes**.
3. A Poisson process looks the same run backwards, so the time since the last bus is also $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$: mean **10 minutes**.
4. Your gap is $10 + 10 = 20$ minutes on average, twice the typical gap. In general (renewal theory) the expected residual wait is
$$\frac{E[X^2]}{2E[X]} = \frac{2/\lambda^2}{2/\lambda} = \frac1\lambda.$$

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** "Gaps average 10 minutes and I land in the middle of one: 5 minutes."

**Why it fails.** You're more likely to arrive during a **long** gap: a 30-minute gap catches six times as many arrivals as a 5-minute one. Gaps you land in are length-biased.

**The trick.** Sample gaps by where *you* fall, not one per bus. The inspection paradox explains why your bus always seems late and why surveyed class sizes look bigger than average.
