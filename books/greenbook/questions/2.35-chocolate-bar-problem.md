## Problem

A chocolate bar has 6 rows and 8 columns of squares. Each break splits one rectangular piece into two along a grid line. How many breaks are needed to get all 48 individual squares?

## Solution

Each break turns one piece into two:
$$\#\text{pieces} \mathrel{+}= 1 \text{ per break.}$$

From $1$ piece to $mn = 48$ pieces takes $mn - 1 = 47$ breaks, whatever the order.

(The book also gives a strong-induction proof: breaking $m\times n$ into $m\times n_1$ and $m\times(n-n_1)$ takes $1 + (mn_1 - 1) + (m(n-n_1)-1) = mn-1$ breaks.)

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Plan the best breaking order: rows first? Columns first?

**Why it fails.** Every order gives the same count, so optimising is pointless.

**The trick.** Track a quantity that changes by a **fixed amount** per move (pieces $+1$). Same idea as the coin split: an invariant makes the path irrelevant.
