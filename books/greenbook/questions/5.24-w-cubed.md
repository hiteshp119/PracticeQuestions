## Problem

Let $W_t$ be a Brownian motion. Is $W_t^3$ a martingale?

## Solution

1. Itô with $f(w) = w^3$, $f' = 3w^2$, $f'' = 6w$:
$$\begin{aligned}d(W_t^3) &= 3W_t^2\,dW_t + \tfrac12\cdot6W_t\,dt\\ &= 3W_t^2\,dW_t + 3W_t\,dt.\end{aligned}$$
2. The drift $3W_t\,dt$ is nonzero whenever $W_t\ne0$, so $W_t^3$ is **not** a martingale.
3. **Fix.** Subtract the accumulated drift: $W_t^3 - 3\int_0^t W_s\,ds$ is a martingale. Using $d(tW_t) = W_t\,dt + t\,dW_t$, another form is
$$W_t^3 - 3tW_t\quad\big(d(W^3 - 3tW) = (3W^2 - 3t)\,dW\big).$$

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** "$W^3$ is an odd function of a symmetric process, so it has mean 0 and must be a martingale."

**Why it fails.** As in card 5.23, a constant mean doesn't make a martingale. Given $W_s = w > 0$, convexity pushes the forecast of $W_t^3$ above $w^3$: $E[W_t^3\mid W_s = w] = w^3 + 3w(t-s)$.

**The trick.** The Itô correction $\frac12f''\,dt$ is the drift. Martingales of Brownian motion are exactly the functions with $f_t + \frac12f_{ww} = 0$: $w$, $w^2 - t$, $w^3 - 3tw$, … (Hermite polynomials).
