## Problem

A European put on a non-dividend stock with strike \$80 costs \$8; a put on the same stock with the same maturity and strike \$90 costs \$9. Is there an arbitrage?

## Solution

1. Put prices are convex in $K$ with $P(0) = 0$, so for $\lambda\in(0,1)$:
$$P(\lambda K) \le \lambda P(K) + (1-\lambda)P(0) = \lambda P(K),$$
strictly when the stock can end in between. With $\lambda = \frac89$: $P(80) < \frac89\cdot9 = 8$. The \$80 put is too expensive.
2. **Trade:** sell 9 puts ($K=80$), buy 8 puts ($K=90$). Cost today $= -9\cdot8 + 8\cdot9 = 0$.
3. Payoff at $T$:
   - $S_T\ge90$: nothing exercised, $0$;
   - $80\le S_T<90$: $8(90 - S_T) > 0$;
   - $S_T<80$: $8(90 - S_T) - 9(80 - S_T) = S_T \ge 0$.
4. Zero cost, never negative, positive with positive probability: an arbitrage.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** "Higher strike, higher put price: 9 > 8, fine."

**Why it fails.** Monotonicity is only one constraint. Puts also must be convex in strike, which here forces $P(80) < 8$.

**The trick.** Use the shape of the payoff: a ratio spread anchored at $P(0) = 0$ turns a convexity violation into a payoff that is never negative.
