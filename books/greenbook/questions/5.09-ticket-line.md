## Problem

$2n$ people queue for \$5 tickets in random order: $n$ hold only \$5 bills and $n$ hold only \$10 bills. The seller starts with no change. What is the probability that everyone can be served in order (the seller always has a \$5 for change)?

## Solution

1. \$5 = $+1$, \$10 = $-1$. Orders ↔ paths from $(0,0)$ to $(2n,0)$: $\binom{2n}{n}$ equally likely. We need the path never to go below 0.
2. **Bad paths** touch $-1$. Reflect a bad path across $y = -1$ after its first touch: it now ends at $-2$. This is a bijection between bad paths and **all** paths to $(2n,-2)$, which have $n-1$ ups and $n+1$ downs:
$$\#\text{bad} = \binom{2n}{n-1}.$$
3. Good paths:
$$\begin{aligned}\binom{2n}{n} - \binom{2n}{n-1} &= \binom{2n}{n}\Big(1 - \frac{n}{n+1}\Big)\\ &= \frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}\quad(\text{Catalan}).\end{aligned}$$
4.
$$P = \frac{1}{n+1}.$$

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Count valid orderings directly, case by case.

**Why it fails.** The "never below zero" constraint is global; direct counts get messy fast.

**The trick.** Count the complement with a bijection: flipping the path after its first mistake maps bad paths onto an easy-to-count set. Reflection reappears for Brownian motion (card 5.20).
