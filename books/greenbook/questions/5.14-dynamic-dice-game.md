## Problem

A casino lets you roll a die as many times as you like. Each roll of 1–5 adds that many dollars to your pot; a 6 loses the whole pot and ends the game. After any roll you may stop and keep the pot. As a risk-neutral player, how much would you pay to play?

## Solution

1. **When to stop.** Holding $n$, one more roll then stopping gives
$$\tfrac16\sum_{k=1}^5(n+k) + \tfrac16\cdot0 = \tfrac56 n + 2.5,$$
better than $n$ iff $n < 15$. Since the pot only grows while you play, this one-step rule is optimal: **stop at $n\ge15$**.
2. **Values.** For $n\ge15$ you stop: $V(n) = n$ (the pot can reach at most $14 + 5 = 19$). For $n\le 14$:
$$V(n) = \tfrac16\big(V(n+1) + \dots + V(n+5)\big).$$
3. Backwards: $V(14) = \frac{15+\dots+19}6 = 14.17$, $V(13) = 13.36$, …, down to
$$V(0)\approx 6.15.$$

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** "Each roll has expected gain $\frac{1+2+3+4+5}{6} = 2.5$, so keep rolling forever."

**Why it fails.** The expected gain ignores the loss: a 6 costs you the whole pot, which is $\frac n6$ in expectation and grows with $n$.

**The trick.** Compare marginal gain ($2.5$) with marginal expected loss ($\frac n6$): stop where they cross. Then backward induction prices the game. Same structure as an American option's early-exercise boundary.
