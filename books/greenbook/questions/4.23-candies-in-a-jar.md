## Problem

A jar holds 10 red, 20 blue and 30 green candies. You take them out one at a time in random order. What is the probability that at the moment you take the last red candy, at least one blue and at least one green are still in the jar?

## Solution

1. Let $T_r, T_b, T_g$ be the positions of the last red, blue and green candies. We need $T_r < T_b$ and $T_r < T_g$: red is **not** among the last two colours to finish. Two orders: $T_r<T_b<T_g$ or $T_r<T_g<T_b$.
2. $T_r < T_b < T_g$: the last candy (position 60) is green, probability $\frac{30}{60}$. Given that, among the 30 red and blue candies the last one is blue with probability $\frac{20}{30}$:
$$\tfrac{30}{60}\cdot\tfrac{20}{30} = \tfrac13.$$
3. $T_r < T_g < T_b$: last candy blue, $\frac{20}{60}$; then among 40 red+green the last is green, $\frac{30}{40}$:
$$\tfrac{20}{60}\cdot\tfrac{30}{40} = \tfrac14.$$
4.
$$P = \tfrac13 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac7{12}\approx 0.583.$$

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Count arrangements of 60 candies with a combinatorial formula.

**Why it fails.** The counting is heavy and easy to get wrong.

**The trick.** Read the sequence **backwards**: a random order reversed is still random, and "last of each colour" becomes "first of each colour". Each new candy from the end is uniform among those not yet seen, so a two-step conditioning finishes it.
