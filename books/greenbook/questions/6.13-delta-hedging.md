## Problem

You buy a European call on GM stock and want to hedge the exposure to GM's price. How do you hedge? If GM then jumps up, how do you rebalance?

## Solution

1. **Hedge:** short $\Delta = e^{-yT}N(d_1)$ shares per call ($N(d_1)$ without dividends): the position's value no longer moves to first order in $S$.
2. The call is worth $c = SN(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$: it behaves like $N(d_1)$ shares financed by borrowing $Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$. Your hedge is the opposite: short the shares, lend the cash.
3. **After a jump up:** $d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K)+\dots}{\sigma\sqrt T}$ increases, so $\Delta = N(d_1)$ increases (gamma $>0$). Short **more** shares (and lend more cash).
4. After a drop, buy shares back. You sell high and buy low, which is what makes a long-gamma position profit from realized volatility (card 6.17).

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Short one share per call.

**Why it fails.** The call moves less than one-for-one with the stock (except deep in the money), so you'd be over-hedged.

**The trick.** Hedge the **slope** of the option's value curve, and keep re-matching it as the curve bends (gamma). A delta hedge is only locally right; big jumps need gamma hedging with other options.
