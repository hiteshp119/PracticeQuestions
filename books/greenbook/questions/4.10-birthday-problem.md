## Problem

Assuming 365 equally likely birthdays, what is the smallest class size for which the probability that two people share a birthday exceeds $\frac12$?

## Solution

1. Complement: all $n$ birthdays distinct.
$$\begin{aligned}P_n &= \frac{365\cdot364\cdots(365-n+1)}{365^n}\\ &= \prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big(1 - \frac{k}{365}\Big).\end{aligned}$$
2. Approximate with $1 - x\approx e^{-x}$:
$$\begin{aligned}P_n&\approx \exp\Big(-\frac{n(n-1)}{730}\Big) < \frac12\\ &\iff n(n-1) > 730\ln2 \approx 506\\ &\iff n\ge 23.\end{aligned}$$
3. Exactly: $P_{22}\approx 0.524$, $P_{23}\approx0.493$. Answer: **23**.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** "You need about $365/2 \approx 183$ people."

**Why it fails.** That is the answer to "someone shares **my** birthday". Here any pair counts, and $n$ people form $\binom n2$ pairs: 253 pairs at $n = 23$.

**The trick.** Count pairs: each pair matches with probability $\frac1{365}$, so collisions become likely once $\binom n2\approx 365\ln 2$, i.e. $n\approx\sqrt{2\cdot365\ln2}\approx 22.5$. Collisions scale like $\sqrt{\text{number of values}}$ (the same idea behind hash collisions).
