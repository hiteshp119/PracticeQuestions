## Problem

$N$ gas cans are placed around a one-way circular track. Together they hold exactly enough fuel for one lap. Your car starts with an empty tank, but you choose where to start. Can you always choose a start that completes the lap?

## Solution

**Approach 1: minimum of a walk.** Pretend you start anywhere with enough borrowed fuel. Let $L(t)$ be the tank level as you drive. It rises at each can by the can's fuel and falls along the road; after a full lap $L$ returns to its starting value (total fuel = lap length). Start instead at the can where $L$ is **lowest**: relative to that point every later level is $\ge 0$, so you never run dry.

**Approach 2: induction (the book's proof).** With cans $x_1,\dots,x_N$ (fuel, as distance) and gaps $y_1,\dots,y_N$ to the next can:
$$\sum x_i = \sum y_i = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; \exists\, i:\ x_i \ge y_i.$$
Can $i$ always reaches can $i+1$, so merge them into one can of $x_i + x_{i+1}$ at position $i$. That's the $N-1$ problem; the base case $N=1$ (start at the can) is trivial.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Start at the biggest can.

**Why it fails.** The biggest can may be followed by a huge empty stretch.

**The trick.** The tank level is a **cumulative sum that returns to its start**; starting at its minimum, all later partial sums are $\ge 0$. This is the same idea as the "gas station" coding problem, solved in $O(N)$ time.
