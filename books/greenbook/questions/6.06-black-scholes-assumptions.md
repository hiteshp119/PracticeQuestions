## Problem

What are the assumptions behind the Black–Scholes formula
$$\begin{aligned}c &= SN(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2),\\ p &= Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2) - SN(-d_1)?\end{aligned}$$

## Solution

1. The stock follows geometric Brownian motion, $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$, with **constant** $\mu$ and $\sigma$ (log returns normal, continuous paths, no jumps).
2. The risk-free rate $r$ is constant and known.
3. No dividends during the option's life (extensions handle a continuous yield $y$: replace $S$ by $Se^{-yT}$).
4. No transaction costs or taxes; trading is continuous; short selling is allowed and its proceeds can be invested.
5. Securities are perfectly divisible.
6. No riskless arbitrage.
7. European exercise only.

With a continuous dividend yield $y$:
$$\begin{aligned}c &= Se^{-yT}N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2),\\ d_{1,2} &= \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r - y\pm\frac12\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T}.\end{aligned}$$
($y = r$ for futures, $y = r_f$ for currencies.)

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** List "normal returns" and stop.

**Why it fails.** The formula also needs the *hedging* assumptions: frictionless continuous trading is what makes the hedge riskless.

**The trick.** Derive the PDE (card 6.05) and note every step that needs an assumption. Real markets break several (vol smile, jumps, costs), which is why traders quote implied volatility rather than trust a single $\sigma$ (card 6.18).
