## Problem

Explain why it is never optimal to exercise an American call on a non-dividend-paying stock before maturity.

## Solution

**Argument 1 (parity).**
$$\begin{aligned}c &= S - Ke^{-rT} + p\\ &= \underbrace{(S - K)}_{\text{intrinsic}} + \underbrace{K\big(1 - e^{-rT}\big)}_{\text{interest on }K} + \underbrace{p}_{>0}\\ &> S - K.\end{aligned}$$
An American call is worth at least $c$, so selling beats exercising.

**Argument 2 (replication).** Instead of exercising at $t$ (getting $S - K$), short one share and lend $K$, keeping the call:

| | now | $S_T \le K$ | $S_T > K$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| call | 0 | 0 | $S_T - K$ |
| short stock | $S$ | $-S_T$ | $-S_T$ |
| lend $K$ | $-K$ | $Ke^{r\tau}$ | $Ke^{r\tau}$ |
| total | $S - K$ | $Ke^{r\tau} - S_T > 0$ | $K(e^{r\tau} - 1) > 0$ |

Same cash today, plus a positive amount later.

**Argument 3 (convexity).** Under the risk-neutral measure, Jensen gives $e^{-r\tau}E[(S_T - K)^+]\ge e^{-r\tau}(Se^{r\tau} - K)^+ \ge S - K$.

**Puts are different:** exercising a deep in-the-money put gets you $K$ now instead of later, which can be worth more than the remaining optionality. With dividends, early exercise of a call can be optimal just before an ex-dividend date.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** "Exercise when the stock is high, to lock in the profit."

**Why it fails.** You can lock in the profit by shorting the stock (or selling the option) and still keep the option's protection and the interest on $K$.

**The trick.** Exercising a call means paying $K$ early and giving up insurance. With no dividend to capture, there's no reason to pay early.
