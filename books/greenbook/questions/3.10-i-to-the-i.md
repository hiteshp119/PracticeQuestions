## Problem

Compute $i^i$.

## Solution

1. **Euler's formula** from Taylor series. With $(i\theta)^2 = -\theta^2$, $(i\theta)^3 = -i\theta^3$, …:
$$\begin{aligned}e^{i\theta} &= \sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(i\theta)^n}{n!}\\ &= \underbrace{\Big(1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2!} + \frac{\theta^4}{4!}-\cdots\Big)}_{\cos\theta}\\ &\quad + i\underbrace{\Big(\theta - \frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \cdots\Big)}_{\sin\theta}.\end{aligned}$$
2. At $\theta = \pi/2$: $e^{i\pi/2} = \cos\frac\pi2 + i\sin\frac\pi2 = i$, so $\ln i = i\pi/2$.
3. Then
$$i^i = e^{i\ln i} = e^{i\cdot i\pi/2} = e^{-\pi/2}\approx 0.2079.$$

**Caveat.** $i = e^{i(\pi/2 + 2\pi k)}$ for every integer $k$, so $i^i$ can be $e^{-\pi/2 - 2\pi k}$; all are real. $e^{-\pi/2}$ is the principal value ($k=0$).

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** "An imaginary number to an imaginary power must be complex."

**Why it fails.** Powers act on the *log*: $a^b = e^{b\ln a}$. $\ln i$ is purely imaginary, and imaginary × imaginary is real.

**The trick.** Put the base in polar/exponential form first. Every complex power becomes $e^{(\text{something})}$, and the rest is arithmetic.
