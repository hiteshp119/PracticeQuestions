## Problem

Estimate the delta of an at-the-money European call on a non-dividend stock. What happens to it as the option approaches expiry?

## Solution

1. $S = K\Rightarrow\ln(S/K) = 0$:
$$d_1 = \frac{(r + \frac12\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T} = \Big(\frac r\sigma + \frac\sigma2\Big)\sqrt T > 0.$$
2. So $\Delta = N(d_1) > \frac12$, and larger for longer maturities. Example: $r = 5\%$, $\sigma = 25\%$, $T = 1$: $d_1 = 0.325$, $\Delta\approx0.63$.
3. As $T\to0$: $d_1\to0$, $\Delta\to N(0) = \frac12$.
4. Away from the money, short-dated deltas move to their limits fast: $\Delta\to1$ for $S\gg K$, $\Delta\to0$ for $S\ll K$. At expiry delta is a step function at $K$.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** "At the money it's 50-50, so $\Delta = 0.5$."

**Why it fails.** Under the stock measure the stock drifts up by $r + \frac12\sigma^2$, so finishing above $K$ is more likely than not.

**The trick.** Look at $d_1$'s formula at $S = K$; everything scales with $\sqrt T$. Same reasoning for a call with yield $y$ if $r > y$.
