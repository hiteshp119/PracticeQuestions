## Problem

For European and American calls and puts, how does the option price change when each of these increases (others held fixed): stock price $S$, strike $K$, time to maturity $T$, volatility $\sigma$, risk-free rate $r$, dividends $D$?

## Solution

1. **$S$ and $K$.** Call payoff $(S_T-K)^+$ rises with $S$, falls with $K$; put payoff $(K-S_T)^+$ the reverse.
2. **$\sigma$.** Payoffs are convex in $S_T$: more spread raises the expected payoff (downside is capped at 0). All four ↑.
3. **$r$.** A call holder pays $K$ later, worth $Ke^{-rT}$ today: higher $r$ makes that cheaper, call ↑. A put holder *receives* $K$ later: put ↓. (Under risk-neutral pricing the stock also drifts up faster.)
4. **Dividends.** The stock drops by the dividend on the ex-date: call ↓, put ↑.
5. **$T$.** American options: a longer option has every right the shorter one has, so ↑. European: ambiguous. A call expiring just before a large dividend can beat one expiring after it; a deep in-the-money European put can be worth less with more time (you wait longer to receive $K$).

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Memorize the table.

**Why it fails.** Under pressure rows get swapped; and "more time is always worth more" is wrong for Europeans.

**The trick.** Decompose: call ≈ long stock, short a bond paying $K$, plus an insurance-like convexity (put–call parity, card 6.02). Each row then follows from "what happens to the stock part, the bond part, and the convexity part". All other things equal is a strong assumption: in practice $r$ and $S$ move together.
