## Problem

You have 12 identical-looking balls. Exactly one is defective: heavier **or** lighter, you don't know which. With a pan balance that only says which side is heavier, find the defective ball and whether it is heavy or light in **3 weighings**.

## Solution

**Counting first.** $24$ possibilities ($12$ balls $\times$ H/L), and $3$ weighings give $3^3 = 27 \ge 24$ outcomes. It's tight, so every weighing must split the cases about evenly into 3.

**Weighing 1:** $\{1,2,3,4\}$ vs $\{5,6,7,8\}$.

**Case "=":** the bad ball is in $9$–$12$; 1–8 are good.
- **Weighing 2:** $\{9,10\}$ vs $\{8,11\}$.
  - "<": 9L, 10L or 11H → weigh 9 vs 10 (lighter one is bad; balance ⇒ 11H).
  - ">": 9H, 10H or 11L → weigh 9 vs 10 (heavier one is bad; balance ⇒ 11L).
  - "=": it's 12 → weigh 12 vs 1 to learn H or L.

**Case "<"** (the case ">" is the mirror image): suspects are 1–4 light or 5–8 heavy, 8 cases.
- **Weighing 2:** $\{1,2,5\}$ vs $\{3,6,9\}$ (9 is known good).
  - "<": 1L, 2L or 6H → weigh 1 vs 2 (lighter is bad; balance ⇒ 6H).
  - ">": 3L or 5H → weigh 5 vs 9 (heavier ⇒ 5H; balance ⇒ 3L).
  - "=": 4L, 7H or 8H → weigh 7 vs 8 (heavier is bad; balance ⇒ 4L).

**General result.** With $n$ weighings you can handle up to $\dfrac{3^n-3}{2}$ balls when heavy/light is unknown (12 for $n=3$), and $3^n$ balls when you know the bad ball is heavy.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Halve it: 6 vs 6, then 3 vs 3…

**Why it fails.** A balance has **three** outcomes, and halving uses only two of them. Halving also never uses the information "it balanced".

**The trick.** Think in information: each weighing should leave about a third of the cases. The second weighing *moves* balls between pans (1,2 stay; 5 switches sides; 3 and 6 come in) so the three outcomes separate light-suspects from heavy-suspects.
