## Problem

An island has 13 red, 15 green and 17 blue chameleons. When two chameleons of different colours meet, both change to the third colour. Can all chameleons ever be the same colour?

## Solution

Let the counts be $(r, g, b)$. A meeting of, say, red and green gives
$$(r,g,b) \to (r-1,\ g-1,\ b+2).$$

Mod 3, $+2 \equiv -1$, so **every count drops by 1 mod 3**:
$$(r,g,b) \to (r-1,\ g-1,\ b-1) \pmod 3.$$

Hence each difference $r-g,\ g-b,\ b-r \pmod 3$ is **invariant**.

- Start: $(13,15,17) \equiv (1,0,2) \pmod 3$, so all pairwise differences are nonzero mod 3.
- One colour, e.g. $(45,0,0)$, needs two counts equal, so a difference $\equiv 0$. Impossible.

**No.**

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Try sequences of meetings by hand and hope to reach $(0,0,45)$.

**Why it fails.** You can't prove "never" by trying.

**The trick.** Look for what's **conserved mod something**. Changes are $(-1,-1,+2)$, and the obvious modulus making $+2 \equiv -1$ is 3. With starting counts $(13,16,16)$ the answer flips to yes.
