## Problem

What happens to the gamma of an at-the-money European call or put as it approaches maturity?

## Solution

1. Calls and puts with the same strike have the same gamma (parity: $c - p$ is linear in $S$):
$$\Gamma = \frac{\partial^2c}{\partial S^2} = \frac{N'(d_1)}{S\sigma\sqrt T} > 0.$$
2. At the money, $d_1 = (\frac r\sigma + \frac\sigma2)\sqrt T\to0$, so $N'(d_1)\to\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}$, while $S\sigma\sqrt T\to0$:
$$\Gamma\to\infty.$$
3. Away from the money ($|\ln(S/K)|$ fixed), $|d_1|\to\infty$ and $N'(d_1)$ decays like $e^{-d_1^2/2}$, faster than $\sqrt T$: $\Gamma\to0$.
4. Picture: delta sharpens into a step at $K$. Short-dated ATM options have the highest gamma; for far OTM/ITM options, longer maturities have higher gamma.

**Consequence.** A small move near the strike close to expiry swings delta from ~0 to ~1, so delta hedging needs huge, frequent rebalancing ("pin risk").

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** "Less time, less option value, so less of every Greek."

**Why it fails.** Value shrinks, but its curvature concentrates at the strike.

**The trick.** Picture the payoff hockey stick: the option value smooths it; as $T\to0$ the smoothing vanishes and all the curvature piles up at the kink.
