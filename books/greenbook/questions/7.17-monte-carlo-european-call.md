## Problem

Explain how to use Monte Carlo simulation to price a European call option.

## Solution

1. Under the risk-neutral measure, $dS = rS\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$. Exact discretization over $\Delta t$:
$$S_{t+\Delta t} = S_t\exp\Big(\big(r - \tfrac12\sigma^2\big)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\varepsilon\Big)$$
with $\varepsilon\sim N(0,1)$.
2. A European payoff depends only on $S_T$, so one step of size $T$ is enough (use many steps for path-dependent payoffs: Asian, barrier, …).
3. Simulate $M$ independent $S_{T,k}$ and average the discounted payoff:
$$\hat c = e^{-rT}\frac1M\sum_{k=1}^M\max(S_{T,k} - K, 0).$$
4. **Error.** $\hat c$ is unbiased; its standard error is $\frac{s}{\sqrt M}$ ($s$ = sample std of discounted payoffs): 100× more paths for one more digit. Variance reduction (card 7.19) helps.
5. **Limits.** Plain Monte Carlo handles path dependence and many underlyings well, but not early exercise directly (American options need regression-based methods such as Longstaff–Schwartz).

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Simulate with the real-world drift $\mu$.

**Why it fails.** Prices are risk-neutral expectations; using $\mu$ gives a forecast, not a price.

**The trick.** Pricing = averaging. Monte Carlo replaces an integral by a sample mean; its error doesn't grow with the number of dimensions, which is why it wins for multi-asset and path-dependent products.
