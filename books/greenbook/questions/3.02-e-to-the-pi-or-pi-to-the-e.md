## Problem

Without computing them, decide which is larger: $e^\pi$ or $\pi^e$.

## Solution

1. $\ln$ is increasing, so compare logs, then divide by $e\pi > 0$:
$$\begin{aligned}e^\pi > \pi^e &\iff \pi \ln e > e\ln \pi\\ &\iff \frac{\ln e}{e} > \frac{\ln \pi}{\pi}.\end{aligned}$$
2. Let $f(x) = \dfrac{\ln x}{x}$, $x>0$:
$$f'(x) = \frac{1 - \ln x}{x^2}\;\begin{cases} >0 & x<e \\ =0 & x = e \\ <0 & x>e\end{cases}$$
3. So $f$ has its global maximum at $x = e$, and $f(e) > f(\pi)$. Hence
$$e^\pi > \pi^e.$$

**Alternative (Taylor).** $e^{y} > 1 + y$ for $y \ne 0$. With $y = \pi/e - 1$:
$$e^{\pi/e - 1} > \frac{\pi}{e} \;\Rightarrow\; e^{\pi/e} > \pi \;\Rightarrow\; e^\pi > \pi^e.$$

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Estimate both numbers: $e^\pi \approx 23.1$, $\pi^e \approx 22.5$.

**Why it fails.** The gap is under 3%; by hand that is easy to get wrong, and the interviewer wants a reason.

**The trick.** Put each number in "per-unit" form: $a^b$ vs $b^a$ is decided by $\frac{\ln a}{a}$ vs $\frac{\ln b}{b}$. The function $\frac{\ln x}{x}$ is a hill with its top at $e$, so $e$ "beats" every other base: $e^x > x^e$ for all $x \ne e$.

**Going further.** The same hill explains $2^4 = 4^2$: $2$ and $4$ sit at equal heights on opposite sides of $e$.
