## Problem

Split 1000 coins into two piles of $x$ and $y$ coins and write down $xy$. Keep splitting every pile, each time adding the product of the two new pile sizes to the running sum, until every pile has one coin. What is the final sum? Show it doesn't depend on how you split.

## Solution

Let $f(n)$ be the final sum for $n$ coins. Small cases: $f(1) = 0,\ f(2) = 1,\ f(3) = 3,\ f(4) = 6$. Guess $f(n) = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$.

**Strong induction.** Suppose it holds below $N$. The first split $N = x + (N-x)$ gives
$$f(N) = x(N-x) + \frac{x(x-1)}{2} + \frac{(N-x)(N-x-1)}{2} = \frac{N(N-1)}{2}.$$
(Expand: $x(N-x) + \tfrac12\big[x^2 - x + (N-x)^2 - (N-x)\big] = \tfrac12 (N^2 - N)$.)

So $f(1000) = \dfrac{1000\cdot999}{2} = 499{,}500$.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Try a few splitting strategies on 1000 coins and compare.

**Why it fails.** That shows it's the same for those strategies, not for all.

**The trick.** **Counting proof:** when you split $x$ and $y$, the product $xy$ counts the **pairs of coins** that are separated right now. Every pair of coins gets separated exactly once by the end, so the total is the number of pairs, $\binom{n}{2}$, independent of the order.
