## Problem

You hold one share of stock A and short $h$ shares of stock B. Returns have volatilities $\sigma_A$, $\sigma_B$ and correlation $\rho$. What $h$ minimizes the variance of your position?

## Solution

1.
$$\begin{aligned}V(h) &= \operatorname{Var}(r_A - hr_B)\\ &= \sigma_A^2 - 2h\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B + h^2\sigma_B^2.\end{aligned}$$
2. First-order condition:
$$\begin{aligned}V'(h) = -2\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B + 2h\sigma_B^2 &= 0\\ \Rightarrow\; h^* &= \rho\frac{\sigma_A}{\sigma_B}.\end{aligned}$$
3. $V''(h) = 2\sigma_B^2 > 0$: a minimum.
4. Remaining variance: $V(h^*) = \sigma_A^2(1-\rho^2)$.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Hedge one-for-one ($h = 1$), or match volatilities ($h = \sigma_A/\sigma_B$).

**Why it fails.** Only the **correlated** part of B can offset A; the rest of B's volatility adds risk.

**The trick.** $h^* = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(r_A,r_B)}{\operatorname{Var}(r_B)}$ is the slope of regressing $r_A$ on $r_B$ (the "beta"). The hedge removes the fraction $\rho^2$ of the variance, the regression $R^2$.
