## Problem

Interest rates are zero; a non-dividend stock is at \$1. A contract pays \$1 the first time the stock price hits \$$H$ ($H>1$), if ever. What is the contract worth today?

## Solution

**Martingale.**
1. With $r = 0$, $S_t$ is a $\mathbb Q$-martingale. Stop at $\tau$ = first hit of $H$ (paths that never hit $H$ drift to 0).
2. $E[S_\tau] = S_0 = 1$, with $S_\tau\in\{H, 0\}$ in the limit:
$$P\cdot H + (1-P)\cdot0 = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; P = \frac1H.$$
3. No discounting: price $=\frac1H$.

**Static hedge.** $\frac1H$ shares cost $\frac1H$ today and are worth exactly \$1 at the moment $S$ first hits $H$; sell them then. If $H$ is never hit, the shares are still worth something, so this portfolio dominates the contract: price $\le\frac1H$. The martingale argument shows the bound is attained (the shares' extra value on never-hit paths has zero expectation in the limit).

**Note.** Under GBM, $\ln S$ has negative drift $-\frac12\sigma^2$, so the stock "usually" goes to 0, yet $E[S_t] = 1$: rare huge paths carry the mean.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Compute the first-passage distribution of GBM and integrate.

**Why it fails.** Possible but heavy, and the answer doesn't even depend on $\sigma$.

**The trick.** Find a traded asset that pays the same at the stopping time: $\frac1H$ shares. Model-free.
