## Problem

Briefly explain value at risk (VaR). What are its potential drawbacks for measuring the risk of derivatives?

## Solution

1. **Definition.** For confidence $\alpha$ (95%, 99%) and horizon $h$, VaR is the loss level $L$ with
$$P(\text{loss} > L) = 1 - \alpha,$$
i.e. a quantile of the P&L distribution.
2. **Tail blindness.** VaR says nothing about how large losses are beyond the quantile. For skewed payoffs (short options, credit) the risk is in that tail. Example: selling protection on a \$1M bond with a 3% default probability (no recovery): 95% VaR $= 0$, though you can lose \$1M.
3. **Not sub-additive.** Add a second, independent \$1M position with 3% default:
$$\begin{aligned}P(\text{at least one default}) &= 1 - 0.97^2 = 5.91\% > 5\%\\ \Rightarrow\; \text{VaR}_{95}(A+B) &= 1\text{M} > 0 + 0.\end{aligned}$$
Diversification appears to *increase* risk, violating a coherence axiom.
4. **Coherent measures** satisfy sub-additivity, positive homogeneity, monotonicity and translation invariance. Expected shortfall (CVaR, average loss beyond VaR) is coherent; stress tests complement VaR for tail risk.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** "VaR is the worst loss you can have."

**Why it fails.** It's the best of the bad outcomes at a given probability, not the worst case.

**The trick.** Quantiles jump at thresholds. Any risk measure that only looks at whether a probability crosses 5% can be gamed by positions whose losses sit just inside the threshold.
