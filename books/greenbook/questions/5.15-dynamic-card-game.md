## Problem

A 52-card deck (26 red, 26 black) is shuffled and the dealer turns cards one at a time. You win \$1 for every red card and lose \$1 for every black card turned. You can tell the dealer to stop at any time. What is the optimal stopping rule and how much is the game worth?

## Solution

1. **State** $(b,r)$ = black and red cards left. Cards drawn so far: $26 - r$ red and $26 - b$ black, so your profit now is $b - r$.
2. **Recursion** (stop now, or draw one more):
$$\begin{aligned}V(b,r) = \max\Big(&b - r,\\ &\tfrac{b}{b+r}V(b-1,r) + \tfrac{r}{b+r}V(b,r-1)\Big).\end{aligned}$$
3. **Boundaries.** $V(0, r) = 0$ (only reds left: draw them all to reach $0$); $V(b,0) = b$ (only blacks left: stop).
4. Fill the $27\times27$ grid from small $b + r$ upward:
$$V(26,26)\approx 2.62.$$
The stopping region is "stop when your profit $b - r$ is large relative to the cards left" (see the widget).

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** "The deck is fair, so the game is worth 0."

**Why it fails.** You choose when to stop. Whenever you're ahead you can lock it in, and if you're behind you can play on to the end, which always brings you back to 0. That option has value.

**The trick.** Optimal stopping = compare "stop now" with "expected value of continuing", computed backwards from states where the answer is obvious. It is the same equation as pricing an American option on a binomial tree.
