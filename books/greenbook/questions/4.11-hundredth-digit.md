## Problem

What is the 100th digit to the right of the decimal point of $(1+\sqrt2)^{3000}$?

## Solution

1. Binomial expansion:
$$(1\pm\sqrt2)^n = \sum_{k}\binom nk(\pm\sqrt2)^k.$$
Adding the two, odd $k$ cancel and even $k$ give integers $\binom nk 2^{k/2}$:
$$N := (1+\sqrt2)^{n} + (1-\sqrt2)^{n}\in\mathbb Z.$$
2. With $n = 3000$ (even), $(1-\sqrt2)^{3000} = (\sqrt2-1)^{3000} > 0$ and
$$(\sqrt2-1)^{3000} = 10^{3000\log_{10}0.4142}\approx 10^{-1148}.$$
3. So $(1+\sqrt2)^{3000} = N - \varepsilon$ with $0<\varepsilon<10^{-1100}$: the decimal part is $0.999\ldots9\ldots$ for over 1100 places. The 100th digit is **9**.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Estimate $(1+\sqrt2)^{3000}$ with logs.

**Why it fails.** Logs give the leading digits; the 100th decimal of a 1149-digit number is far beyond any approximation.

**The trick.** Conjugates. $1+\sqrt2$ and $1-\sqrt2$ are the roots of $x^2 - 2x - 1$; powers of one are nearly integers because the other root's powers vanish. The same trick shows $\varphi^n$ is close to a Lucas number.
