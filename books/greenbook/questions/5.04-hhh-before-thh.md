## Problem

You toss a fair coin until either HHH or THH appears. What is the probability that HHH appears first?

## Solution

1. If the first three tosses are HHH (probability $\frac18$), HHH wins.
2. Otherwise some toss $t$ is a T before HHH has appeared. HHH contains no T, so it can only finish using an HH that lies after $t$. Take the **first** HH after $t$, at tosses $j, j+1$. The toss before it, $j-1$, is a T (either $t$ itself, or else an H at $j-1$ would make an earlier HH). So THH is complete at toss $j+1$, while HHH needs at least toss $j+2$.
3.
$$P(\text{HHH first}) = \tfrac18.$$

**Markov check.** States $S, H, HH, T, TH$: from $T$ or $TH$ you can never reach HHH first (a T just returns to $T$), so $a_T = a_{TH} = 0$, and $a_S = \frac12a_H = \frac14a_{HH} = \frac18$.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** "Both triples are equally likely, so $\frac12$."

**Why it fails.** The patterns compete inside one sequence. THH's tail (HH) is HHH's head, so THH "steals" every HH that follows a T.

**The trick.** Ask how each pattern can *begin* to form and who finishes first. This non-transitive behaviour is the basis of Penney's game (card 5.05).
