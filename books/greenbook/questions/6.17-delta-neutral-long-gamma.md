## Problem

You are long a call on a non-dividend stock and short enough shares to be delta-neutral. If the stock price jumps up or down immediately, what happens to the value of your portfolio? Is this an arbitrage?

## Solution

1. Taylor expand the portfolio $\Pi$ (stock has zero gamma):
$$\delta\Pi\approx\underbrace{\Delta_\Pi}_{=0}\delta S + \tfrac12\Gamma(\delta S)^2 + \Theta\,\delta t.$$
2. An **immediate** move ($\delta t\approx0$) gives $\delta\Pi\approx\frac12\Gamma(\delta S)^2 > 0$: you gain whichever way the stock moves.
3. **Not an arbitrage.** BSM PDE with $\Delta_\Pi = 0$:
$$\Theta + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\Gamma = r\Pi \;\Rightarrow\; \Theta = r\Pi - \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\Gamma.$$
With large positive gamma, theta is negative: if the stock doesn't move, you bleed. Over $\delta t$, the expected gain $\frac12\Gamma\sigma^2S^2\delta t$ exactly pays for the decay at the implied $\sigma$.
4. Net P&L per step $\approx\frac12\Gamma S^2\big(\text{realized return}^2 - \sigma_{\text{implied}}^2\delta t\big)$: you win if realized vol exceeds implied.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** "I make money on any move: free money."

**Why it fails.** The market charges for convexity through time decay.

**The trick.** Gamma and theta are two sides of one trade: you're long variance at the implied level. Gamma scalping turns this into the delta re-hedging of card 6.13: sell after rises, buy after falls.
