## Problem

What is the price of a cash-or-nothing European call (pays \$1 if $S_T > K$) on a non-dividend stock following GBM? How would you hedge it, and what is the limitation?

## Solution

1. **Price.** Pays $\mathbf 1_{S_T>K}$:
$$c_{\text{bin}} = e^{-rT}\,\mathbb Q(S_T > K) = e^{-rT}N(d_2).$$
Note $-\frac{\partial c}{\partial K} = e^{-rT}N(d_2)$: a digital is minus the strike-derivative of a vanilla call (card 6.20).
2. **Delta hedge.**
$$\Delta = \frac{\partial c_{\text{bin}}}{\partial S} = e^{-rT}N'(d_2)\frac{1}{S\sigma\sqrt T}.$$
Fine far from $K$ or far from expiry. But at $S\approx K$ and $T\to0$, $\Delta\to\infty$: tiny moves demand huge trades. Practically unhedgeable near expiry.
3. **Static hedge.** A call spread long $\frac1{2\varepsilon}$ calls at $K - \varepsilon$, short $\frac1{2\varepsilon}$ at $K + \varepsilon$ pays 0 below $K - \varepsilon$, 1 above $K + \varepsilon$, a ramp in between. As $\varepsilon\to0$ it replicates the digital, but you need those strikes and a large position $\frac1{2\varepsilon}$. Desks usually over-hedge with a slightly wider spread on the conservative side.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Delta-hedge like any other option.

**Why it fails.** The payoff is a step; its delta near expiry is a spike, so continuous rebalancing breaks down.

**The trick.** Approximate the step by a ramp you can build from vanilla calls. The steeper the ramp, the closer the hedge and the bigger the position.
