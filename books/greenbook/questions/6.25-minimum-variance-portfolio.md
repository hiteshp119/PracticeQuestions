## Problem

Stocks A and B both have expected return 12%. A has volatility 20%, B has 30%, and their correlation is 0.5. How should you split your money to minimize the portfolio's risk?

## Solution

1. Weights $w$ in A, $1-w$ in B; expected return is $12\%$ for any $w$, so only variance matters:
$$V(w) = \sigma_A^2w^2 + \sigma_B^2(1-w)^2 + 2\rho\sigma_A\sigma_Bw(1-w).$$
2. $V'(w) = 0$:
$$\begin{aligned}w^* &= \frac{\sigma_B^2 - \rho\sigma_A\sigma_B}{\sigma_A^2 - 2\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B + \sigma_B^2}\\ &= \frac{0.09 - 0.03}{0.04 - 0.06 + 0.09} = \frac{0.06}{0.07} = \frac67.\end{aligned}$$
3. Invest $\frac67\approx85.7\%$ in A and $\frac17$ in B. Volatility: $\sqrt{V(\frac67)}\approx19.6\%$, a little below A alone.

**General Markowitz:** minimize $w^\top\Sigma w$ s.t. $w^\top\mu = \mu_p$, $w^\top e = 1$; Lagrange multipliers give $w^* = \Sigma^{-1}(\lambda e + \gamma\mu)$.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** "Put everything in the less volatile stock A."

**Why it fails.** B isn't perfectly correlated with A, so a small B position diversifies some of A's risk away.

**The trick.** The optimum is where the marginal variance from each asset is equal. Adding B helps until $\operatorname{Cov}(r_p, r_B) = \operatorname{Cov}(r_p, r_A)$. With $\rho\ge\sigma_A/\sigma_B$ you'd hold no B at all (here $0.5 < 0.67$).
