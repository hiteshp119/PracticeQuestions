## Problem

Write down the Black–Scholes–Merton differential equation and explain how to derive it.

## Solution

1. Stock: $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$. Itô on $V(S,t)$:
$$dV = \Big(V_t + \mu SV_S + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}\Big)dt + \sigma SV_S\,dW.$$
2. Portfolio $\Pi = V - V_S S$ (long the option, short $\Delta = V_S$ shares). Over $dt$, holding $\Delta$ fixed:
$$d\Pi = dV - V_S\,dS = \Big(V_t + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}\Big)dt.$$
No $dW$, no $\mu$: riskless.
3. No arbitrage: a riskless portfolio earns $r$:
$$\Big(V_t + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}\Big)dt = r\big(V - SV_S\big)dt.$$
4. Rearranged:
$$V_t + rSV_S + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS} = rV.$$
In Greek letters: $\Theta + rS\Delta + \frac12\sigma^2S^2\Gamma = rV$.

**Feynman–Kac view.** $V(t,S) = E^{\mathbb Q}\big[e^{-r(T-t)}f(S_T)\mid S_t = S\big]$ with $dS = rS\,dt + \sigma S\,dW^{\mathbb Q}$ solves exactly this PDE with $V(T,S) = f(S)$.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Discount the expected payoff under the real drift $\mu$.

**Why it fails.** $\mu$ depends on risk preferences; the hedging argument shows the price can't depend on it.

**The trick.** Hedging removes the randomness, and a riskless position must earn $r$. That single fact replaces $\mu$ by $r$ ("risk-neutral pricing").
