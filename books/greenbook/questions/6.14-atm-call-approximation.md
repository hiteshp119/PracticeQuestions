## Problem

Estimate the value of an at-the-money European call on a non-dividend stock when interest rates are low and the maturity is short. Apply it to $S = K = 100$, $\sigma = 20\%$, $T = 0.25$ years.

## Solution

1. $r\approx0$, $S = K$: $c\approx S\big(N(d_1) - N(d_2)\big)$ with
$$d_1 = \tfrac12\sigma\sqrt T,\qquad d_2 = -\tfrac12\sigma\sqrt T.$$
2. Both are near 0, where $N$ is nearly linear with slope $N'(0) = \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}$:
$$N(d_1) - N(d_2)\approx\frac{\sigma\sqrt T}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.$$
3. So
$$c\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sigma S\sqrt T\approx 0.4\,\sigma S\sqrt T.$$
4. Example: $0.4\times0.2\times100\times\sqrt{0.25} = 4.0$ (exact Black–Scholes with $r = 0$: $3.99$).

Traders invert it: implied vol $\approx\frac{c}{0.4\,S\sqrt T}$. The same estimate prices an ATM put, and an ATM straddle at $\approx0.8\sigma S\sqrt T$.

## Intuition

**Naive approach.** Pull out a normal table and compute $d_1$, $d_2$ exactly.

**Why it fails.** Interviews want a mental estimate.

**The trick.** At the money with no drift, the call is the expected positive part of a normal move: $E[X^+] = \frac{s}{\sqrt{2\pi}}$ for $X\sim N(0, s^2)$, with $s = \sigma S\sqrt T$.
